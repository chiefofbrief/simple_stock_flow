PER-TICKER NARRATIVES

MA (Mastercard)
MA is the standout in this group and not close. The spread (-38.5%) is powered by genuine, durable earnings growth — EPS up 21% on 1Y and 35% on 2Y — while price has fallen 17% over the past year after being up 9% over two years. That pattern (recent dislocation against a positive 2Y backdrop) is exactly what the framework targets: an acute event overriding a fundamentally intact business. The P/E correlation of -0.84 is the most negative in the group, confirming that the divergence has been persistent and systematic throughout the year, not just a single quarter's artifact. What separates MA from V — with whom it shares an identical P/E of 27.8x — is what happens below the P/E line: MA's P/OE is 24.6x (below P/E, meaning owner earnings exceed net income after SBC), FCF is growing +28% vs_1Y and +120% vs_2Y, and ROIC at 91% is nearly double V's and rising +14pp in the past year alone. A business compounding owner earnings at 20%+ with a 91% ROIC and minimal leverage (Debt/OCF 1.04x) doesn't often trade at 27.8x GAAP earnings after a 17% price decline. The key open question: what triggered the sell-off, and is it exogenous (macro, tariff-related payment volume fears) or specific to Mastercard? The answer determines whether the dislocation is recoverable or a leading indicator of fundamental pressure.

V (Visa)
V presents a strong spread signal with a more complicated quality picture than the surface numbers suggest. EPS growing 35% on 1Y and 37% on 2Y while price is down 12% — and the P/E correlation of -0.71 confirms the divergence has been persistent. The business is exceptional by conventional measures: ROIC 49% and rising, revenue growing 17–28% across both periods, Debt/OCF 1.05x. What makes V rank below MA despite a larger spread is the FCF anomaly: FCF is declining -40% on both 1Y and 2Y, at the same time that EPS is growing 35% and revenue is growing 17%. Earnings quality (OCF/NI: 1.02x) looks clean, which raises the question of where the FCF erosion is coming from — heavy capex, working capital changes, or cash used in buybacks not flowing through to the FCF line. A 35% EPS growth rate alongside a -40% FCF decline over two consecutive periods is a contradiction that should be resolved before conviction. V and MA are structurally similar businesses, but MA's FCF growing +120% over two years vs. V's falling -38% over the same window is a real distinction. The 27.8x GAAP P/E is the same for both; MA gets you substantially better economics for the same multiple. The single most important open question: what is driving FCF to decline sharply while EPS grows strongly, and is this a structural shift in the business model or a transient deployment of capital?

DG (Dollar General)
DG has the hallmarks of a LOSER candidate at the price level: 12.4% EPS growth while price declined 5.3% this year, GAAP P/E of 14.7x — cheap by the framework's benchmark — and P/OE of 8.3x, which is exceptionally low and implies the market is pricing this at roughly 8x owner earnings. OCF/NI of 2.88x is a real data point about cash generation but requires explanation: for a capital-intensive retailer, large depreciation charges will inflate OCF relative to net income structurally, and the P/OE accounts for SBC already. The concerning elements are two. First, ROIC is 7.4% — below the 10% threshold, meaning the business may be marginally destroying value on capital it reinvests. The ROIC trend is flat (+0.2pp over two years), not improving. Second, FCF fell -34.5% this year after growing from the 2Y base. Revenue grew only 3.4% this year. A discount retailer with flat ROIC, decelerating revenue growth, and a sharp FCF decline is not obviously "high quality with a solvable problem" — it may be a structurally challenged business priced cheap for good reason. That the stock is also down on both 1Y (-5.3%) and 2Y (-19.2%) means the dislocation is not recent or acute; it has been building for two years. The key open question: is the FCF decline a capex cycle (investment that will earn future returns) or evidence of operational deterioration, and at 7.4% ROIC, are there any signs of franchise strengthening?

PATH (UiPath)
The -206.6% spread is entirely a base effect, not a signal. EPS went from approximately $0.20 to $0.60 TTM — a 197% gain that reflects recovery from near-zero profitability, not a compounding franchise. The Avg EPS QoQ figure of +3,159% confirms the noise: a business oscillating around breakeven produces infinite-percentage swings on small absolute moves. The P/E correlation of -0.03 tells the story directly — price and earnings have had essentially no relationship for a year, which means there is no systematic dislocation to exploit. The valuation surface is also misleading: GAAP P/E of 19.6x looks reasonable until the P/OE of 58.5x is read alongside it. The gap between the two implies roughly $267M of annual SBC being applied to a business with $375M of gross FCF — SBC consumes approximately 71% of free cash flow. Shareholders are meaningfully diluted by the equity compensation structure even as reported EPS improves. Revenue growth (17.3%) and FCF growth (21.7%) are genuine positives, and if the SBC trajectory were declining as a share of revenue, there would be a case for watching this name. But at below $10B market cap with zero P/E correlation, no systematic dislocation, and SBC consuming most of FCF, this does not meet the bar for tracker inclusion.

VRRM (Verra Mobility)
The spread of -67.8% looks like a strong signal until the price data is read: -82.8% on 1Y and -83.8% on 2Y. This is not an acute recent dislocation — it is a sustained multi-year collapse in price with no sign of stabilization at the 2Y horizon. The framework's spread signal is designed to identify temporary mispricings; a stock down 84% on a 2-year basis warrants structural explanation, not a dislocation thesis. The quality data compounds the concern: ROIC jumped +53pp to 58.5% while FCF fell -78% over the same year. These two figures cannot both be accurate reflections of economic reality — a business generating 58.5% returns on capital would produce growing, not collapsing, cash flows. The almost certain explanation is an invested capital denominator effect: write-offs or impairments reduced book equity and debt on the balance sheet, inflating the ROIC calculation mechanically without any improvement in cash generation. FCF declining -54.6% over two years alongside a -83.8% price decline and -15% EPS decline is the actual picture of the business — not a compelling one. Below $10B market cap and a price decline matching the depth of the FCF decline both argue this is priced down for operational reasons that the spread calculation obscures.
