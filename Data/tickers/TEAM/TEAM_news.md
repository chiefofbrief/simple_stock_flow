### 2026-04-19 | Atlassian plans Jira, Confluence AI collection for 300,000
**Sentiment:** +0.01 (pos: 0.24, neg: 0.23, neu: 0.54)

Atlassian says that starting August 17, 2026, it will use customer metadata and in-app content from Jira, Confluence, and other Atlassian Cloud products to train its AI models, including Rovo and Rovo Dev, affecting about 300,000 customers. The company will treat two categories differently: de-identified, aggregated “metadata” (such as readability and complexity signals, task classifications, story points, sprint end dates, and SLA values) will be collected by default for most users, while “in-app data” (such as Confluence page titles and bodies and Jira issue titles, descriptions, comments, and custom field names) is enabled by default only for some tiers. Free and Standard customers cannot opt out of metadata (and in-app data can be configured), Premium keeps metadata always on but has in-app data off by default, and Enterprise turns both on-by-default off with opt-out options; some customers—such as those on customer-managed encryption keys, Atlassian Government/Isolated Cloud, or with HIPAA obligations—are excluded from the data collection altogether. Atlassian says it will retain collected data for up to seven years and, if users opt out or delete content, will remove relevant in-app data within 30 days and retrain affected models within 90 days to purge contributions. The policy reverses Atlassian’s earlier stance that customer data would not be used to train or improve its AI services. Critics argue the change creates a “pay-for-privacy” dynamic in which most users have limited ability to fully opt out unless they buy the top tier or fall under specific legal constraints.

**Key Points:**
- Atlassian’s head of product communications, Arseny Tseytlin, said in an email to The Register that for customers whose highest active plan is Free, Standard, or Premium, “metadata contribution is always on, and they are not able to opt out,” adding that Atlassian “remove[s] information that directly identifies individuals, such as name and email addresses.”
- Tseytlin also argued that keeping metadata “de-identified and aggregated at a customer level and is common across customers” “enables us to make more meaningful observations over longer periods of time” and helps “drive continual improvements to [the] overall experience,” framing the retention as product-improvement rather than individual surveillance.
- Atlassian said training on this data will improve specific user-facing capabilities—for example, enabling apps to “surface the most relevant results” for prompts/queries, “summariz[e] relevant content more accurately and concisely,” and “identify[] the best templates for creating new documents.”

Developer analytics firms say this “tokenmaxxing” can inflate early signals like code “acceptance,” since tools such as Claude Code, Cursor, and Codex generate far more code that engineers initially approve. **Key Points:**
- Several articles argue that token-budget metrics may be promoted because they “might make sense if you’re trying to encourage more AI adoption (or selling tokens), but not if you’re trying to become more efficient,” i.e., the incentives behind tracking tokens can diverge from true productivity.
- Beyond acceptance/rewrite churn, one report specifies what gets measured upstream in many teams: “input parameters” such as “the number of requests, token spend, [and] generation time,” which can inflate apparent productivity without reflecting user value.
- Waydev’s platform update is described as going beyond churn/quality scoring to “track metadata created by AI agents” and publish analytics on the “quality” impact—suggesting the company is capturing agent-level signals, not only repo-level outcomes.
