# Fair Isaac (FICO) — Q3 FY2026 Breakdown

*What the company is, in one line: FICO sells the credit score that about 90% of U.S. lenders use to decide who gets a loan and at what rate — charging per score pulled — plus a growing software business that helps banks automate lending, fraud, and risk decisions.*

## Synthesis and Takeaways

- **The quality of the business and the strength of its moat are elite and not really in question; the argument is entirely about whether it can keep growing this fast.** For every dollar of sales, about 52 cents is operating profit and about 42 cents turns into actual spare cash, and the company earns roughly 77 cents of profit a year for every dollar tied up in the business (a return on invested capital of 76.5%, which is exceptional and has risen five years running) (FICO_financial_analysis.md). Crucially, the reported profit is backed by real cash — cash flow is actually a bit *higher* than reported net income — so there is no accounting trick inflating the earnings (FICO_financial_analysis.md; 10-Q).
- **Growth is fast but slowing, and almost all of it is higher prices, not more customers.** Total revenue grew +26% in Q3 (down from +39% in Q2); the core Scores business grew +41%; and mortgage-score revenue specifically grew +97% — yet the *number* of mortgage scores sold grew only low-single-digit (Q3 2026 call). In other words, FICO is charging much more per score while selling roughly the same quantity. That is enormously profitable today, but it is also exactly what draws competitive and regulatory fire.
- **The mortgage moat looks genuinely hard to break, and management can show why.** When Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac released their own loan data, an independent actuarial firm (Milliman) found FICO's newest score (10T) predicts default better than the rival VantageScore on all three standard measures — using the *same underlying data* — so FICO's edge is decades of modeling skill, not privileged data (Q3 2026 call). The rival's share of the government-backed ("conforming") mortgage market is structurally capped in the low-20s%, is about 2% everywhere outside conforming mortgages, and is 0% in the non-government ("nonconforming") market. Bond investors have accepted only ~20 mortgages *ever* built on the rival's score (Q2/Q3 2026 call Q&A).
- **The competitive threat is real but contained — and the deeper risks are dependence and pricing durability.** "Gaming" (a lender pulls both scores and submits whichever one gets the borrower a better rate) is confirmed at Rocket and UWM, but because it requires FICO to be pulled too, it *adds* to FICO's volume rather than replacing it (Q3 2026 call Q&A). The bigger structural worries: the three credit bureaus that resell FICO's scores also co-own the rival, and they are now 63% of FICO's revenue; and Florida's attorney general has opened an antitrust probe aimed squarely at the pricing power that produced that +97% (news, 2026-07-04).
- **The one place quality and safety part ways is the balance sheet.** Unlike a debt-free software company, FICO deliberately borrowed heavily to buy back its own stock: $5.58B of debt at a 5.64% average rate against just $305M of cash, with more equity paid out than remains on the books (Q3 2026 call). Debt is about 5.6x annual operating cash flow, a loan-agreement limit ("covenant") steps down to 3.5x over time, and roughly $2.4B comes due in 2028 and must be repaid or refinanced. Cash flow covers the interest several times over, so this is a constraint, not a crisis — but it removes the cushion for a bad year.
- **The stock got much cheaper as earnings rose, and the next catalyst is a coin-flip on timing.** It trades near 27x earnings (about 31x on strict accounting), versus a five-year typical level above 50x, and sits about 46% below its 52-week high near $1,998 (news, 2026-06-08; market data). The swing factor is a new mortgage pricing program (the "Direct License Program") that is built, wanted by lenders, and waiting on a single government sign-off — but it has already slipped past management's own timeline. The next earnings report (~Nov 4) is the read on whether the price-only growth engine can keep running.

Put together, this looks like paying about 70 to 75 cents for a dollar of value. The profitability, the returns, and the mortgage moat are elite and the price has genuinely come down, but the growth is now a pure pricing story on flat volume, the event that would re-accelerate it (the new pricing program) keeps getting delayed, and the very pricing power driving the numbers is what invites the antitrust case and the conflict with its own distributors — so it is a strong risk/reward, not a sure thing.

## Growth

The headline is strong in size, slowing in trend, and — unlike most software companies — driven almost entirely by raising prices rather than adding customers or volume.

The engine is the **Scores** segment (selling credit scores), which is 68% of revenue and grew +41% in Q3 at a 91% profit margin (Q3 2026 call). Zoom in and the picture is even more concentrated: mortgage-score revenue alone grew +97% year over year and is 62% of all Scores revenue, while the actual *number* of mortgage scores sold grew only low-single-digit, "consistent with the bureaus" (i.e., in line with the overall mortgage market) (Q3 2026 call Q&A). So nearly all the growth is a higher price per score, not more scores. This is the single most important fact about the business: it is spectacularly high-margin and cash-generative, but it is a pricing story with a natural ceiling, on a product that already has ~90% market share.

The slowdown is real, and it matters *where* you read it. Total revenue bounces around quarter to quarter because the software side books some one-time license revenue unevenly, so the cleaner signal is the Scores/mortgage line:

- Scores segment growth: **+60% (Q2 FY2026) → +41% (Q3 FY2026)**
- Mortgage-score revenue growth: **+127% (Q2) → +97% (Q3)**
- Total revenue growth: **+39% (Q2) → +26% (Q3)**; full-year guidance **+20%** (Q2/Q3 2026 calls)

One important clarification on that deceleration: it is a tough prior-year comparison and a softer mortgage market, not lost share. Management said plainly that Q2's +127% was flattered by prior-year pricing timing and an unusually strong-volume quarter, and that Q3 cooled because "as rates tick up, volumes slow down" — the same softness the credit bureaus reported (Q3 2026 call Q&A). The audited numbers show no sign of customers leaving for VantageScore; the slowdown is the housing market plus a hard comparison.

Beyond simply raising the mortgage rack rate again, there are two forward growth levers:

1. **The Direct License Program (DLP) / FICO 10T** — a new way to charge. Instead of a high fee every time a score is pulled, FICO cut the up-front price to $0.99 per score and added a $65 "funding fee" charged only when a loan actually closes (revised down from an initial $4.95 + $33). This matches the rival's $0.99 entry price while making money on completed loans instead (Q2 2026 call). Management says it earns roughly the same either way; lenders covering ~60% of mortgage volume have signed on (with a path to ~90%); and it is fully built and waiting on one government certification to go live — a real catalyst, but one that has been "almost there" for several quarters (Q3 2026 call).
2. **Software / Platform** — the subscription business. Reported software revenue grew only +2%, but that hides a healthy shift: the modern cloud "Platform" product grew +66%, its recurring revenue base ("ARR," annual recurring revenue — the run-rate of subscription revenue) grew +62% to $413M and passed the old legacy business for the first time, and existing Platform customers spent 48% more than a year earlier (a "net retention rate" of 148%) (Q3 2026 call). The catch is that the old on-premise software is shrinking (−17%), which drags the blended software number down and squeezes the software margin during the transition.

Worth flagging: FICO gave **no FY2027 guidance** and did not put a number on long-term growth. The durability question the results cannot yet answer is whether the new pricing program can keep the Scores engine growing once the current mortgage repricing runs its course — because volume, on its own, is basically flat.

## Profitability

This is the strongest and least controversial part of the story — and, importantly, it is not new or fragile.

- **Margins are elite, steady, and structurally rising.** Operating margin (profit left after running costs) is 51.7% for the trailing year and 53.8% in Q3, up every single year from 38.4% in 2021, with very little wobble (FICO_financial_analysis.md). By the company's preferred adjusted measure it was 62% in Q3. The Scores segment alone runs a 91% margin; the software segment runs 26% (down from 32%). Because Scores keeps growing into a bigger share of the whole, the company-wide margin rises mechanically — which is also the tell that this margin story leans heavily on Scores.
- **Free cash flow is huge and genuine.** Free cash flow (spare cash after all spending) is about $1.0B for the trailing year, roughly 42 cents of every revenue dollar; the company frames it as $961M over the last four quarters, up 28% (FICO_financial_analysis.md; Q3 2026 call). Capital spending is almost nothing (~0.2% of revenue), because selling a credit score requires no factories or heavy equipment — so nearly all cash flow is truly free.
- **The earnings are real, not accounting flattery.** Operating cash flow is 1.23x reported net income and has been steady for five years, meaning the profit shows up as actual cash. Stock-based pay (giving employees shares instead of cash, which quietly dilutes owners) is only 7.3% of revenue and shrinking — low for software (FICO_financial_analysis.md; 10-Q). The one blip — a weak cash quarter in Q2 — was simply the timing of customer payments after the pricing surge, and it fully reversed in Q3.
- **Returns on capital are exceptional and getting better.** Return on invested capital — profit earned per dollar of money tied up in the business — is 76.5%, up from 41% in 2021 (FICO_financial_analysis.md). Part of that headline number is flattered by the buyback-shrunk capital base, but even measured conservatively the business earns far more than its cost of capital, and the trend is up. In plain terms: the moat shows up directly in how much profit each dollar of investment throws off.
- For scale, next to PTC (a strong software company used here only as a yardstick, not a competitor): FICO's 51.7% margin and 76.5% return crush PTC's 37.8% and 26.7%, because selling a score costs almost nothing to produce (FICO_financial_analysis.md).

Two honest caveats. First, those eye-popping 91%/26% segment margins leave out company-wide costs and stock pay, so the all-in, apples-to-apples number is the 51.7% figure — not the segment number (10-Q segment note). Second, unlike a cash-rich software peer, this profitability sits on a heavily borrowed balance sheet (see Risk), so "very profitable" and "financially bulletproof" are not the same thing here.

## Risk

The economics are not the risk. The risks are whether the pricing-led growth lasts, how dependent FICO is on a few partners who are also rivals, regulation aimed right at the pricing power, and a balance sheet loaded with debt.

- **Growth is price on flat volume — and the price is what everyone is attacking.** With mortgage volumes flat and FICO already at ~90% share, growing means charging more per score, which is precisely what Florida's attorney general is investigating: a legal demand alleging predatory pricing, forced bundling, and exclusionary contracts with the bureaus, noting FICO's price rose "from under $1 to as much as $10 per score" (news, 2026-07-04). A U.S. senator and an earlier antitrust lawsuit from banks are part of the same overhang. This is the most direct threat, because it targets the one lever actually driving growth.
- **Its distributors are also its rival's owners — and that dependence is deepening.** The three big credit bureaus (Experian, TransUnion, Equifax) resell FICO's scores and generated 63% of Q3 revenue, up from 54% a year ago. Those same bureaus jointly own VantageScore. So the more successfully FICO raises mortgage prices, the more motivated its own sales channel becomes to push the competing product it owns — a conflict that worsens with every price hike (Q3 2026 call Q&A).
- **VantageScore is a contained rival, not a scale threat — but it is the clearest external warning.** VantageScore appeared in ~30% of Q2 mortgage credit *pulls* (news, 2026-07-31), which sounds alarming until you realize that is "gaming": lenders pull both scores and keep whichever helps the borrower, which requires FICO to be pulled too, so it adds to FICO rather than replacing it, and the rival's ceiling in conforming mortgages is structurally in the low-20s% (Q3 2026 call Q&A). Outside conforming mortgages the rival is ~2% and mostly given away free by the bureaus; in nonconforming mortgages it is 0%. The durability case rests on two things: FICO's score is more predictive on the same data, and the rival's data only goes back to 2013 and has never been tested through a full boom-and-bust credit cycle — so bond investors demand extra yield to hold mortgages scored on it (Q2 2026 call Q&A).
- **AI is a long-term tail risk by product line, not a near-term disruptor.** Fair-lending law requires lenders to *explain* every credit decision (FICO provides 32 standardized "reason codes"), you cannot legally use certain predictive-but-discriminatory factors, and regulators (the CFPB) already shut down one AI "black-box" lender (Upstart) — all of which slows AI-native scoring in regulated lending, and FICO holds 137 patents in explainable/ethical AI (Q2 2026 call Q&A). The moat is strongest in mortgages and weakest in personal loans and credit cards, where fintech lenders lean on their own AI models and use FICO more as a legal safe-harbor.
- **The key catalyst keeps slipping.** The new pricing program has been "closing in on it" for multiple quarters; management candidly admitted "this much time…is more than we expected; we actually believed it would be up and running by now" (Q2 2026 call Q&A). It is real but unscheduled, and a slip into FY2027 leaves the stock waiting.
- **The debt removes the margin for error.** $5.58B of debt at 5.64%, only $305M of cash, debt at 5.6x operating cash flow, negative book equity (more capital paid out than remains), a loan covenant tightening to a 3.5x debt-to-earnings limit, and roughly $2.4B due in 2028 that must be repaid or refinanced (Q3 2026 call; 10-Q notes). Cash flow covers the interest many times over and management has shifted to paying down debt before buying more stock (Q3 2026 call Q&A) — but a bad year would bite faster than at a debt-free peer.
- **Sentiment is cooling at the edges.** Wolfe Research downgraded the stock on the Q3 revenue miss (news, 2026-08-05), price targets have drifted down over the year, and a director sold about two-thirds of her stake under a pre-set plan (news, 2026-08-05) — small individually, but part of a more cautious tone.

## Narrative

FICO spent the last year being re-rated on fear rather than results, and the whole argument has narrowed to one question: can a company that grows mainly by raising prices keep it up. The business is a near-monopoly credit score (~90% share) with a 91% margin and a 76.5% return on capital, wrapped around a fast-growing software platform that helps banks automate decisions. For most of 2025 investors happily paid more than 50x earnings for that. Then the mortgage repricing cycle produced eye-popping Scores growth (+127% mortgage revenue in Q2), the stock ran up, and the price investors will pay per dollar of earnings has since fallen by nearly half — to about 27x — even as the earnings themselves kept climbing (news, 2026-06-08). Profits compounded ~26% a year over five years while the valuation shrank; the two roughly cancel, which is why the stock sits near its five-year *average price* despite a much bigger business underneath.

Then came the Q3 wobble. Revenue came in less than 1% light while earnings beat and guidance was raised — but the market fixated on the slowdown (mortgage revenue growth easing from +127% to +97%, total from +39% to +26%) and Wolfe Research cut its rating over competitive worries (news, 2026-08-05). Management's explanation was specific and credible: volumes are flat because mortgage rates ticked up, the deceleration is a hard prior-year comparison plus a soft housing market, and the audited numbers show no share loss (Q3 2026 call Q&A). That reassures on the moat but confirms the bears' real point — the growth is price, not volume, so it depends on charging more or on getting the new pricing program live.

The competitive and regulatory backdrop sharpened that same tension. The rival showing up in ~30% of mortgage credit pulls (news, 2026-07-31) looks threatening until you see it is gaming that still requires FICO to be pulled, and that the government's own released data — checked independently by Milliman — shows FICO's score predicts default better on identical inputs (Q3 2026 call). Meanwhile Florida's antitrust probe went straight at the pricing power (news, 2026-07-04), and the bureaus that both distribute FICO and co-own the rival now supply 63% of its revenue — the deepening dependence that is the real structural risk.

Sentiment is split, and that split is the setup. Value-minded buyers and most analysts see a cheap, elite compounder — Seeking Alpha ran "This Compounder Is On Sale" (June 17) and "Strong Earnings Growth Makes The Valuation Attractive Again" (June 24), and serious long-term institutions (Northwestern Mutual, Akre, Capital Research, Norway's central bank) have been buying (news). On the other side, the sell-side is quietly cooling, targets have drifted lower, and the catalyst that would flip the story — the new mortgage pricing program — has already slipped past management's own deadline. It all compresses into the next report: around November 4, Q4 FY2026 will show whether flat volume plus the new pricing model can keep the price-led engine running, or whether the mortgage repricing cycle is maturing before its next monetization lever is even switched on. Elite economics and a durable mortgage moat sit on one side; pricing-ceiling, partner-dependence, regulatory, and debt risks on the other — and that print is the near-term tiebreaker.
