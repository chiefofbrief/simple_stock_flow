# Fair Isaac (FICO) — Q3 FY2026 Breakdown

*What the company is, in one line: FICO sells the credit score that about 90% of U.S. lenders use to decide who gets a loan and at what rate — charging per score pulled — plus a growing software business that helps banks automate lending, fraud, and risk decisions.*

*Source note: this is a distilled read of the full FICO investment thesis; "(thesis)" refers to `FICO_Thesis.md`, with underlying data (news, analyst, filings, calls) named where it adds specificity.*

## Synthesis and Takeaways

- **The quality of the business and the strength of its moat are elite and not really in question; the argument is entirely about whether it can keep growing this fast.** For every dollar of sales, about 52 cents is operating profit and about 42 cents turns into actual spare cash, and the company earns roughly 77 cents of profit a year for every dollar tied up in the business (a return on invested capital of 76.5%, exceptional and rising five years straight) (thesis, "The Numbers"). Crucially, the reported profit is backed by real cash — cash flow is actually a bit *higher* than net income — so there's no accounting trick inflating earnings (thesis).
- **Growth is fast but slowing, and almost all of it is higher prices, not more customers.** Total revenue grew +26% in Q3 (down from +39% in Q2); the core Scores business grew +41%; and mortgage-score revenue specifically grew +97% — yet the *number* of mortgage scores sold grew only low-single-digit (thesis; Q3 2026 call). FICO is charging much more per score while selling roughly the same quantity. Hugely profitable today, but exactly what draws competitive and regulatory fire.
- **The mortgage moat looks genuinely hard to break, and the thesis can show why.** When Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac released their own loan data, an independent actuarial firm (Milliman) found FICO's newest score (10T) predicts default better than rival VantageScore on all three standard measures — using the *same underlying data* — so FICO's edge is decades of modeling skill, not privileged data (thesis). The rival's share of government-backed ("conforming") mortgages is structurally capped in the low-20s%, is ~2% everywhere outside conforming mortgages, and is 0% in the non-government ("nonconforming") market; bond investors have accepted only ~20 mortgages *ever* built on it (thesis).
- **The competitive threat is real but contained — the deeper risks are dependence and pricing durability.** "Gaming" (a lender pulls both scores and submits whichever gets the borrower a better rate) is confirmed at Rocket and UWM, but because it needs FICO pulled too, it *adds* to FICO's volume rather than replacing it (thesis). The bigger worries: the three credit bureaus that resell FICO's scores also co-own the rival, and they're now 63% of revenue; and Florida's attorney general opened an antitrust probe aimed squarely at the pricing power that produced that +97% (thesis; news, 2026-07-04).
- **The one place quality and safety part ways is the balance sheet.** Unlike a debt-free software company, FICO deliberately borrowed heavily to buy back its own stock: $5.6B of debt against just ~$0.25–0.3B of cash, with more equity paid out than remains on the books (thesis). Debt is about 5.6x annual operating cash flow, a loan-agreement limit ("covenant") tightens to 3.5x over time, and roughly $2.4B comes due in 2028 and must be repaid or refinanced. Cash flow covers interest several times over, so it's a constraint, not a crisis — but it removes the cushion for a bad year (thesis).
- **The stock got much cheaper as earnings rose, and the next catalyst is a coin-flip on timing.** It trades near 27x earnings (about 31x on strict accounting), versus a five-year typical level above 50x, and sits about 46% below its 52-week high near $1,998 (thesis; news, 2026-06-08). Over the past year the price fell while earnings climbed — a −0.78 correlation, the signature of a sentiment repricing rather than a broken business (thesis). The swing factor is a new mortgage pricing program (the "Direct License Program") that's built, wanted by lenders, and waiting on a single government sign-off — but it's already slipped past management's own timeline. The next report (~Nov 4) is the read on whether the price-only engine keeps running.

Put together, this looks like paying about 70 to 75 cents for a dollar of value. The profitability, the returns, and the mortgage moat are elite and the price has genuinely come down, but the growth is now a pure pricing story on flat volume, the event that would re-accelerate it keeps getting delayed, and the very pricing power driving the numbers is what invites the antitrust case and the conflict with its own distributors — so it's a strong risk/reward, not a sure thing (thesis).

## Growth

The headline is strong in size, slowing in trend, and — unlike most software — driven almost entirely by raising prices rather than adding customers or volume (thesis).

The engine is the **Scores** segment (selling credit scores), 68% of revenue, up +41% in Q3 at a 91% profit margin. Zoom in and it's even more concentrated: mortgage-score revenue alone grew +97% year over year and is 62% of all Scores revenue, while the actual *number* of mortgage scores sold grew only low-single-digit, in line with the overall mortgage market (thesis; Q3 2026 call). So nearly all the growth is a higher price per score, not more scores. This is the single most important fact about the business: spectacularly high-margin and cash-generative, but a pricing story with a natural ceiling, on a product that already has ~90% share.

The slowdown is real, and it matters *where* you read it. Total revenue bounces around quarter to quarter because the software side books one-time license revenue unevenly, so the cleaner signal is the Scores/mortgage line:

- Scores segment growth: **+60% (Q2 FY2026) → +41% (Q3 FY2026)**
- Mortgage-score revenue growth: **+127% (Q2) → +97% (Q3)**
- Total revenue growth: **+39% (Q2) → +26% (Q3)**; full-year guidance **+20%** (thesis)

One clarification on that deceleration: it's a tough prior-year comparison and a softer mortgage market, not lost share. Q2's +127% was flattered by prior-year pricing timing and an unusually strong-volume quarter, and Q3 cooled because as rates tick up, volumes slow — the same softness the bureaus reported (thesis; Q3 2026 call). The audited numbers show no sign of customers leaving for VantageScore.

Beyond simply raising the mortgage rack rate again, there are two forward levers:

1. **The Direct License Program (DLP) / FICO 10T** — a new way to charge. Instead of a high fee every time a score is pulled, FICO cut the up-front price to $0.99 per score and added a $65 "funding fee" charged only when a loan actually closes (revised down from an initial $4.95 + $33). This matches the rival's $0.99 entry price while making money on completed loans instead. Management says it earns roughly the same either way; lenders covering ~60% of mortgage volume have signed on (path to ~90%); and it's fully built and waiting on one government certification — a real catalyst that's been "almost there" for several quarters (thesis).
2. **Software / Platform** — the subscription business. Reported software revenue grew only +2%, but that hides a healthy shift: the modern cloud "Platform" product grew +66%, its recurring revenue base ("ARR," the run-rate of subscription revenue) grew +62% to $413M and passed the old legacy business for the first time, and existing Platform customers spent 48% more than a year earlier (a "net retention rate" of 148%). The catch: the old on-premise software is shrinking (−17%), dragging the blended software number down and squeezing the software margin during the transition (thesis).

Worth flagging: FICO gave **no FY2027 guidance** and put no number on long-term growth. The durability question the results can't yet answer is whether the new pricing program can keep the Scores engine growing once the current mortgage repricing runs its course — because volume, on its own, is basically flat (thesis).

## Profitability

The strongest and least controversial part of the story — and, importantly, not new or fragile (thesis, "The Numbers").

- **Margins are elite, steady, and structurally rising.** Operating margin (profit after running costs) is 51.7% for the trailing year and 53.8% in Q3, up every single year from 38.4% in 2021 (62% by the company's adjusted measure). The Scores segment alone runs 91%; software runs 26% (down from 32%). Because Scores keeps growing into a bigger share of the whole, the company-wide margin rises mechanically — which is also the tell that the margin story leans heavily on Scores (thesis).
- **Free cash flow is huge and genuine.** Spare cash after all spending is about $1.0B for the trailing year, roughly 42 cents of every revenue dollar. Capital spending is almost nothing (~0.2% of revenue) — selling a credit score needs no factories — so nearly all cash flow is truly free (thesis).
- **The earnings are real, not accounting flattery.** Operating cash flow is 1.23x net income and steady for five years, so profit shows up as actual cash. Stock-based pay (shares instead of cash, which quietly dilutes owners) is only 7.3% of revenue and shrinking — low for software. And the wider accounting review found no red flags: revenue recognition is clean, nothing aggressive in how costs are capitalized, no accrual games (thesis, "Accounting").
- **Returns on capital are exceptional and improving.** Return on invested capital — profit per dollar tied up in the business — is 76.5%, up from 41% in 2021. Part of the headline is flattered by the buyback-shrunk capital base, but even measured conservatively the business earns far more than its cost of capital, and the trend is up. In plain terms: the moat shows up directly in how much profit each dollar of investment throws off (thesis).
- For scale, next to PTC (a strong software company used only as a yardstick, not a competitor): FICO's 51.7% margin and 76.5% return crush PTC's 37.8% and 26.7%, because selling a score costs almost nothing to produce (thesis).

Two honest caveats. First, those 91%/26% segment margins leave out company-wide costs and stock pay, so the all-in, apples-to-apples number is the 51.7% figure — not the segment number. Second, unlike a cash-rich software peer, this profitability sits on a heavily borrowed balance sheet (see Risk), so "very profitable" and "financially bulletproof" aren't the same thing here (thesis).

## Risk

The economics aren't the risk. The risks are whether the pricing-led growth lasts, how dependent FICO is on partners who are also rivals, regulation aimed right at the pricing power, and a debt-loaded balance sheet (thesis).

- **Growth is price on flat volume — and the price is what everyone is attacking.** With volumes flat and FICO at ~90% share, growing means charging more per score, which is exactly what Florida's attorney general is investigating: a legal demand alleging predatory pricing, forced bundling, and exclusionary bureau contracts, noting FICO's price rose "from under $1 to as much as $10 per score" (thesis; news, 2026-07-04). A U.S. senator and an earlier bank antitrust suit are part of the same overhang. The most direct threat, because it targets the one lever actually driving growth.
- **Its distributors are also its rival's owners — and that dependence is deepening.** The three big bureaus (Experian, TransUnion, Equifax) resell FICO's scores and generated 63% of Q3 revenue, up from 54% a year ago. Those same bureaus jointly own VantageScore. So the more successfully FICO raises mortgage prices, the more motivated its own sales channel becomes to push the competing product it owns (thesis).
- **VantageScore is a contained rival, not a scale threat — but it's the clearest external warning.** VantageScore appeared in ~30% of Q2 mortgage credit *pulls* (news, 2026-07-31), which sounds alarming until you realize that's gaming: lenders pull both and keep whichever helps the borrower, which requires FICO too, so it adds to FICO rather than replacing it, and the rival's ceiling in conforming mortgages is structurally in the low-20s% (thesis). Outside conforming mortgages the rival is ~2% and mostly given away free by the bureaus; in nonconforming it's 0%. The durability case rests on two things: FICO's score is more predictive on the same data, and the rival's data only goes back to 2013 and has never been tested through a full boom-and-bust cycle — so bond investors demand extra yield to hold mortgages scored on it (thesis).
- **AI is a long-term tail risk by product line, not a near-term disruptor.** Fair-lending law requires lenders to *explain* every credit decision (FICO provides 32 standardized "reason codes"), certain predictive-but-discriminatory factors are illegal to use, and regulators (the CFPB) already shut down one AI "black-box" lender (Upstart) — all of which slows AI-native scoring in regulated lending, and FICO holds 137 patents in explainable/ethical AI (thesis). The moat is strongest in mortgages and weakest in personal loans and credit cards, where fintech lenders lean on their own AI models and use FICO more as a legal safe-harbor.
- **The key catalyst keeps slipping.** The new pricing program has been "closing in on it" for multiple quarters; management admitted it's taken "more time than we expected…we actually believed it would be up and running by now" (thesis). Real but unscheduled, and a slip into FY2027 leaves the stock waiting.
- **The debt removes the margin for error.** $5.6B of debt at ~5.6%, only ~$0.25–0.3B of cash, debt at 5.6x operating cash flow, negative book equity, a covenant tightening to a 3.5x limit, and ~$2.4B due in 2028 that must be repaid or refinanced. Cash flow covers interest many times over and management has shifted to paying down debt before buying more stock — but a bad year would bite faster than at a debt-free peer (thesis).
- **Sentiment is cooling at the edges.** Wolfe Research downgraded on the Q3 revenue miss (news, 2026-08-05), price targets have drifted down over the year, and a director sold about two-thirds of her stake under a pre-set plan (news, 2026-08-05) — small individually, but part of a more cautious tone.

## Narrative

FICO spent the last year being re-rated on fear rather than results, and the whole argument has narrowed to one question: can a company that grows mainly by raising prices keep it up. The business is a near-monopoly credit score (~90% share) with a 91% margin and a 76.5% return on capital, wrapped around a fast-growing software platform that helps banks automate decisions. For most of 2025 investors happily paid more than 50x earnings for that. Then the mortgage repricing cycle produced eye-popping Scores growth (+127% mortgage revenue in Q2), the stock ran up, and the price investors will pay per dollar of earnings has since fallen by nearly half — to about 27x — even as the earnings kept climbing. Profits compounded ~26% a year over five years while the valuation shrank; the two roughly cancel, which is why the stock sits near its five-year *average price* despite a much bigger business underneath (thesis).

Then came the Q3 wobble. Revenue came in less than 1% light while earnings beat and guidance was raised — but the market fixated on the slowdown (mortgage revenue easing from +127% to +97%, total from +39% to +26%) and Wolfe Research cut its rating over competitive worries (news, 2026-08-05). The explanation is specific and credible: volumes are flat because mortgage rates ticked up, the deceleration is a hard comparison plus a soft housing market, and the audited numbers show no share loss (thesis). That reassures on the moat but confirms the bears' real point — the growth is price, not volume, so it depends on charging more or getting the new pricing program live.

The competitive and regulatory backdrop sharpens that same tension. The rival showing up in ~30% of mortgage credit pulls (news, 2026-07-31) looks threatening until you see it's gaming that still requires FICO to be pulled, and that the government's own released data — checked independently by Milliman — shows FICO's score predicts default better on identical inputs. Meanwhile Florida's antitrust probe went straight at the pricing power (news, 2026-07-04), and the bureaus that both distribute FICO and co-own the rival now supply 63% of its revenue — the deepening dependence that is the real structural risk (thesis).

Sentiment is split, and that split is the setup. Value-minded buyers and most analysts see a cheap, elite compounder — Seeking Alpha ran "This Compounder Is On Sale" (June 17) and "Strong Earnings Growth Makes The Valuation Attractive Again" (June 24), and serious long-term institutions (Northwestern Mutual, Akre, Capital Research, Norway's central bank) have been buying (news). On the other side, the sell-side is quietly cooling, targets have drifted lower, and the catalyst that would flip the story — the new mortgage pricing program — has already slipped past management's own deadline. It all compresses into the next report: around November 4, Q4 FY2026 shows whether flat volume plus the new pricing model can keep the price-led engine running, or whether the mortgage repricing cycle is maturing before its next monetization lever is even switched on. Elite economics and a durable mortgage moat on one side; pricing-ceiling, partner-dependence, regulatory, and debt risks on the other — and that print is the near-term tiebreaker (thesis).
