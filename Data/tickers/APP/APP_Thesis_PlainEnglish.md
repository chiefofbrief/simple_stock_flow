## How we're looking at this

We're judging AppLovin on the quality and durability of its growth, not on the fact that the stock got cheap. Yes, the stock is down about 55% from its 52-week high, but "it's cheap" is not the reason to like it. The real question is whether the growth is real, high-quality, and likely to last. Cheapness by itself doesn't earn a thumbs-up.

## What the market is saying

**The immediate story is an ugly one: AppLovin missed its own guidance for the first time in ages.** On August 6, 2026, headlines read "AppLovin Misses Q2 Results; Stock Falls." Revenue of about $1.924 billion came in a hair below what Wall Street expected (~$1.935–1.94 billion), and profit (adjusted EBITDA) landed below the company's own promised range. The stock dropped about 20% after hours — its worst day in over a year. Earnings per share of $3.76 did hit the estimate. Management and analysts blamed *timing*: the company grows by constantly improving its AI model, and this quarter the improvement was smaller than usual, with the next big one arriving just after the quarter ended. Needham pointed out the next quarter's guidance already bakes in that improvement, so it's actually more conservative than normal (implying about 7.6% growth quarter-over-quarter). Importantly, this wasn't blamed on customers spending less.

**Before earnings, the conversation had three parts.** People kept saying the stock was expensive (around 38x earnings, pricey versus other software names). People were excited about AppLovin opening its ad platform to the public on June 22 and pushing into e-commerce. And Bank of America spooked everyone on July 13 with data suggesting the e-commerce rollout was slowing (it added 750 new advertiser "pixels" in June versus 950 in May), which knocked the stock down more than 12% in a day and even triggered a securities-fraud investigation by a law firm (Pomerantz).

**Two things pull in opposite directions.** Insiders have been selling heavily — roughly $197 million over three months, including the CEO (Foroughi), the CFO (Stumpf), and several directors. At the same time, big institutions have been buying — funds own about 42% of the company, and several added aggressively (Spyglass grew its stake 38%, Epoch 67%, Dorsey opened a position that's 10% of its whole portfolio). There's also a genuine "this is undervalued" camp: one discounted-cash-flow estimate pegs fair value at $858 versus the ~$470 the stock traded at then (a 45% discount); another model says $508.

**The key takeaway:** the panic is out of proportion to what actually happened. This was a revenue miss of less than 1%, in a quarter where revenue still grew 53% year-over-year at a 65.8% net margin. That looks like the market falling out of love with a former highflier, not a business falling apart. The one genuinely new fact is about *cadence* — the AI-improvement engine that drives growth didn't deliver its usual quarterly boost.

**What the analysts think:** the average analyst price target is $592, which would be about 77% above the ~$336 price; the consensus is $573 (+71%), with targets ranging from $340 to $790. Coverage is deep (53 analysts), so this isn't a thinly-followed name. But conviction is slipping — the average target has been marked down steadily ($668 a year ago → $605 last quarter → $562 last month). In the last 90 days there were 2 downgrades (Wells Fargo and Piper Sandler both cut on the earnings day) against 12 firms holding their bullish ratings. So the pros are trimming a bit at the edges, but they haven't headed for the exits.

## The stock price and the earnings behind it

**AppLovin is very profitable and its earnings have exploded.** Earnings per share went from $0.06 (2023) to $2.35 (2024) to $6.91 (2025) to about $13.01 over the last twelve months. (One caveat: the older EPS numbers include the games business that was later sold, so treat that history as "the company is clearly profitable and growing" rather than as a precise valuation input — the clean, current $13.01 is what we value off of.) This is a real, reliably profitable business, not a company burning cash.

**The stock is basically at its 52-week low** — $335.67, in a range of $332 to $746, down about 55% from the top. It's down 14% from a year ago and sits 35% below its one-year average price of $520. Zoom out five years, though, and price and earnings have both rocketed up together: the stock compounded about 39% a year, and it currently sits 75% above its five-year average price of $191. This is a proven long-term winner — but a wild ride: it once fell 92% (in the 2022 crash) and has had 50+ days of 5%+ moves in the past year alone.

**Over the last twelve months, price and earnings went in opposite directions.** The stock fell while earnings kept climbing. The monthly path down was brutal: $719 (Sep) → $674 (Dec) → $398 (Mar) → back to $613 (May) → $515 (Jun) → $396 (Jul) → $336 (Aug). So this last year was the *valuation* shrinking, not the *earnings* shrinking.

**On valuation:** the stock trades at about 25.7x earnings today (using the clean $13.01 and $335.67), which is a reasonable, even undemanding, multiple for a company still growing revenue over 50% and earnings ~88%. It used to trade around 56x a year ago. The correlation between price and earnings over the past year is −0.67 — meaning price fell while earnings rose, which usually signals an overreaction. The honest read: the market has repriced the stock from 56x to 26x while earnings kept growing, mostly because it doubts the fast growth will *continue*. So the whole bet comes down to one thing — **does the growth hold up?**

## What the company's own filings show

**What drove the quarter:** Q2 revenue was $1.924 billion, up 53%. Net income was $1.267 billion (a 65.8% margin), and adjusted profit (EBITDA) was $1.614 billion (an 83.9% margin, up about 3 points from a year ago). Here's the important nuance: the growth came entirely from making *more money per ad*, not from showing more ads — revenue per install rose 58% while the number of installs actually fell 2%. That's the crux of the whole story: growth comes from the AI model getting better, and those improvements come in lumps.

**The company is now just an advertising business.** It sold its "Apps" (mobile games) division to a company called Tripledot on June 30, 2025, for $400 million cash plus about a 20% stake in Tripledot. So it now reports as one segment — advertising — with product lines AppLovin Ads (most of the revenue), MAX, Adjust, and Wurl (connected-TV).

**Guidance and risks:** the filing itself doesn't give dollar guidance (that comes on the earnings call). The risks it flags are: heavy dependence on Apple's and Google's app stores (they set the rules and fees), and tightening privacy/AI regulation. Worth noting — those aren't what spooked the market; the market is fixated on the near-term growth wobble, while the filing's real long-term risks are the app-store gatekeepers.

**On AI:** AI isn't a side project here — it *is* the business. The revenue engine is the "Axon" AI system that matches advertisers with places to show ads. The AI payoff is already in the numbers (this quarter's entire +53% came from the AI getting better at monetizing). The cost is quantified: about 10 cents of computing cost for every extra $1 of revenue. The Q2 profit miss was because they spent more on computing to train a bigger, better model — and that model went live in Q3. So this is a working, money-making machine, not a science project waiting to pay off. The catch: because growth *is* model improvement, any quarter without a big model step-up slows down.

**Preliminary expectation before digging into the financials:** we expected to find elite economics — gross margins around 88%, adjusted profit margins around 84%, net margins around 65%, free cash flow above 70% of revenue, almost no net debt, and very high returns on capital. And we expected growth still above 50% but slowing (59% → 53% → guided ~47%). The pivotal question: is the slowdown just the natural result of getting big (harder to grow fast when you're already huge), or the first sign the AI-improvement engine is genuinely running out of steam? At ~26x earnings, the price is already assuming a sharp slowdown — so the bet is simply that growth doesn't fall off a cliff.

## The financials, checked in detail

**First, a warning about the data itself.** The "peer" the system picked for comparison — C3.ai — is useless here. It loses money on every line (negative margins, negative cash flow, negative returns). It's technically in the same software category but is nothing like AppLovin, so ignore it.

**More importantly, the data source (FMP) is unreliable for AppLovin around the 2024 games-divestiture, and this matters a lot:**

- *Quarterly problem:* FMP's quarterly revenue is inconsistent. It shows Q1 and Q4 of 2024 as the *old, bigger* company (games included: $1,058M and $1,373M) but Q2 and Q3 as the *new, ads-only* company. That inconsistency is exactly what created a fake signal: the screening file (`Large_Actives_with_Metrics.csv`) shows AppLovin's growth *accelerating* by +38.2 percentage points — a big reason for its high 93.2 growth score. That acceleration is a mirage. On clean, apples-to-apples numbers, growth has actually been *slowing every single quarter* (from +77% to +53%). The growth *level* is genuinely elite; the "it's speeding up" signal was just bad data.

- *Annual problem:* the same divestiture breaks the yearly history too. FMP's annual figures are ads-only for 2023–2025 but the whole old company (games included) for 2020–2022 — you can see it because revenue "falls" from $2.82B (2022) to $1.84B (2023) and then jumps to $3.22B (2024), and the old years even show *losses*. So any five-year average, five-year growth rate, or volatility stat that spans that gap is mixing two different companies and shouldn't be trusted for *any* metric, not just revenue.

- *What we actually relied on:* every valuation and per-share number in this analysis uses the clean, current data — the last four quarters, which are all after the games sale and contain no contamination. The trailing EPS of $13.01, the 25.7x P/E, and the downside math all check out (trailing net income works out to $4.41 billion ÷ ~336 million shares ≈ $13 per share). The contaminated older numbers only ever show up here to be dismissed or to sketch a rough trend. Two things to read as "old-company context only": the yearly cash-flow figures for 2023–2025 technically still include some games cash flow (but the trailing-twelve-month figures we anchor on are clean), and the EPS history above.

Now the actual metrics, all on clean data:

**Revenue:** about $6.83 billion over the last twelve months; $5.48 billion in 2025 (+70%) and $3.22 billion in 2024 (+75%) — two straight years of ~70% growth in the ad business. Quarter by quarter it went $1.41B → $1.66B → $1.84B → $1.92B, and the quarter-over-quarter growth is clearly slowing (+11.6%, +18.0%, +11.1%, +4.4%). All the growth is from making more per ad, not more ads. Bottom line: a genuinely elite top line still growing over 50%, but slowing, and entirely dependent on the AI getting better.

**Operating margin:** about 77% (and rising — it went 42% in 2023 → 59% in 2024 → 76% in 2025). A ~78% operating margin that's still *widening* is extraordinary and is the strongest single sign of a real competitive moat. This is the opposite of "buying growth by cutting prices."

**Operating cash flow:** about $4.53 billion over the last twelve months. It dipped in Q2 (to $0.87B from ~$1.3B), which looks alarming but is just tax and interest payment timing — the company paid $639.8 million in cash taxes in 2025 (versus $194.8 million the year before), weighted to the middle of the year. Not a real deterioration.

**Free cash flow:** about $4.50 billion — roughly 66% of revenue. It's basically equal to operating cash flow because the company spends almost nothing on physical stuff (its servers are leased, costing only ~$22 million a year; the heavy computing is rented from the cloud and shows up as a running cost, not an investment). This is an incredibly cash-rich, asset-light business — and that $4.5 billion of cash generation is a hard floor under the stock.

**Earnings quality:** cash flow roughly equals reported profit (a ratio of about 1.0). That's unusual and *good* for a software company — most software firms' "real" cash is much lower than reported profit because they hand out tons of stock to employees. AppLovin's stock-based pay is small (about 4% of revenue), so its reported earnings are genuinely backed by cash. There's no "the real number is worse" gap to worry about, which is why the P/E can be trusted.

**Working capital / balance sheet health:** the company is piling up cash ($2.5B at end-2025 growing to $3.05B by mid-2026). Money owed to it by customers grew 42% while revenue grew 70% — meaning it's collecting *faster* than it's growing, which is healthy. No single customer is more than 10% of sales. Everything here is a fortress, not a strain.

**Operating leverage:** looks low, but that's because margins are already ~78% — there's almost no fixed cost left to "leverage," so nearly every new dollar already drops through at a high rate. Not a weakness.

**Spending and depreciation:** capital spending is tiny. Depreciation is actually *shrinking* (old acquisition-related charges are burning off), confirming how little physical stuff this business needs. The one real commitment is a $702.8 million cloud-computing contract — but that's a running cost, not owned equipment.

**Debt:** $3.6 billion of fixed-rate bonds (5.1%–6% interest, due 2029–2054) plus an untapped $1 billion credit line. It could pay off *all* its debt with under 10 months of cash flow (a debt-to-cash-flow ratio of 0.78x). Net of its cash, leverage is basically zero. Debt is a non-issue here; it's used to fund stock buybacks, not to survive.

**Return on invested capital:** about 126% over the last twelve months — astronomically high (anything over 20% signals a durable moat). Part of that sky-high number is because buybacks have shrunk the capital base, but even adjusting for that, it's elite. The nuance: because the company reinvests almost nothing (it's asset-light and returns cash via buybacks), its growth comes from *revenue growth times high margins*, not from plowing money back in — which again means the whole story rides on growth holding up.

**A few things dug out of the footnotes:**
- **No backlog and no retention metric.** Customer contracts can be canceled anytime, and revenue is booked per ad shown — so there's no contracted future revenue to give visibility. That's a real structural weakness for judging how durable growth is.
- **A tax perk that expires.** A Singapore tax incentive cut the tax bill by $272 million in 2025 (worth about $0.80 per share) — and it expires in June 2028 unless renewed. That's a real, if modest (~6% of EPS), future headwind.
- **The scary-looking $188.9M goodwill write-down was on the games business being sold**, not the ongoing ad business — so it doesn't touch the numbers that matter.
- Overall the accounting is clean: no aggressive revenue tricks, no cost-hiding, conservative treatment of computing costs, and — unusual for software — a small gap between reported (GAAP) and "adjusted" numbers, meaning management isn't dressing things up.

**Putting the financials together:**
- Is the P/E trustworthy? Yes — unusually so. Because stock comp is low and cash equals profit, the ~26x is an honest multiple with no cheaper "real" number hiding underneath and no inflated adjusted number to discount. On 45%+ growth and 126% returns, 26x is cheap.
- What's the downside? Not the balance sheet — that's bulletproof. The risk is growth. If the AI engine genuinely slows and growth falls toward 20–25%, the market could re-rate the stock to a "slowing grower" multiple of 15–18x, which on ~$13 of earnings means roughly **$195–235** (a 30–42% drop). Add the Singapore tax expiry and the ever-present Apple/Google rule-change risk. The huge cash flow makes a *permanent* wipeout unlikely, but a lower multiple on slowing growth is the live danger.
- What's the free upside? The consumer/e-commerce business is scaling fast (spend was 28% above the seasonal peak), the newly-opened public platform, and lead-gen and connected-TV are all early and barely in the numbers. At 26x, you're paying for a slowdown and getting all of that for free.
- Is AI actually paying off? Decisively yes — it's already the money-maker, margins went *up* even as computing spend rose, and the cost is a manageable, variable ~10 cents per dollar of revenue. The only catch is the lumpiness.

**The financial verdict:** this is a best-in-class, cash-rich, extremely profitable business with a fortress balance sheet — the quality is not in doubt. The only open question is whether the growth stays strong, and that can't be answered from the financials alone (no backlog, no retention data). That's what the earnings call has to address.

## What management said on the earnings calls

**The most important call is the August 5 (Q2) one** — it has the miss, the explanation, a reset of long-term expectations, the crucial next-quarter guidance, and the news that the SEC closed its inquiry. The earlier May call is the "here's our grand vision" call that also set the guidance Q2 went on to miss.

**Management's account matches the filings exactly** ($1.92B revenue, $1.61B profit, $863M cash flow), and to their credit they were blunt: "we fell short of that standard... we know what happened, and it's already been addressed." Three useful things came out of the call that the raw numbers couldn't tell us:
- *Why they missed:* the model-improvement was lighter than usual this quarter and the next big one landed just after the quarter closed. That's the whole miss.
- *Where growth is coming from:* the consumer/e-commerce business set a record, running 28% above last year's holiday peak in a normally-slow quarter, and is growing faster than gaming. Gaming is still the majority of revenue and was the source of the miss. Publisher earnings on their MAX product grew double-digits — so demand is *not* weak.
- *The cash-flow dip was just timing* (tax and interest payments), and they expect it to normalize to ~75% of profit for the year — exactly matching what we figured out independently.

They also proactively announced the SEC's informal inquiry was closed with no action — clearing away that fraud-investigation cloud.

**Guidance going forward:** Q3 revenue of $2.055–2.085 billion (up 46–48% from a year ago, up 7–8% from Q2) and profit of $1.71–1.74 billion. Longer term, they now frame the business as compounding "roughly 30% a year." Here's a subtle but important point: that +47% Q3 number is still a *slowdown* from Q2's +53% and Q1's +59%. When management says "reaccelerating," they mean quarter-over-quarter (7–8% versus Q2's 4.4%) — the year-over-year rate is still declining. And the new "~30% long-term" framing is itself a step down from today's 50%+. It's still elite, but it's a reset lower.

**The tone changed a lot.** In May the CEO was euphoric ("the future has never looked better... we've never been wrong once"). In August he was humble ("this was not the quarter we hold ourselves to"). New in the Q2 call: the SEC closure, the ~30% reset, more emphasis on the computing investment, and a shift in how they'll win consumer advertisers — from "we'll open the doors and they'll come" to "we'll do it through partnerships" (like Triple Whale). And several bits of the May hype quietly shrank: the big "$7 billion opportunity / $70k per customer" math got walked back ("we never expected the public launch to change the business overnight"), the AI video-ad tool that was "days away" is now "still work in progress," and they admitted the brand isn't well-known in e-commerce and that small advertisers are "harder for us to make work right now." Honest, but a yellow flag on *how fast* the consumer opportunity actually arrives.

**On our open questions, here's where the calls landed:**
1. *Is the Q3 rebound real?* Partly — management says the improvement is "already live" and Q3 is "off to a strong start," but that's guidance, not results. The November print is the real test.
2. *Gaming vs. consumer split?* Partly answered — consumer is scaling faster, gaming is still the bulk. No exact dollar split.
3. *Is the model-improvement lumpy?* Answered, and it confirms the worry: management openly said "there's no guarantee we're always going to have lifts in every... 3 months... you're testing hypotheses, hoping for a good result." Growth genuinely *is* lumpy.
4. *Singapore tax / Apple-Google / competition?* The Singapore tax expiry was *not* addressed on either call (still open). Apple/Google wasn't directly addressed. The Unity competition question was — management brushed it off, arguing their data advantage raises the ceiling for everyone.
5. *Computing costs and margins?* Answered — ~10 cents per dollar holds, and they target low-80s% margins long-term, with some short-term wobble.

On the risk side: AI is *not* eroding this business the way it threatens traditional software — AppLovin is the AI winner, not the victim. The extra computing spend did squeeze quarter-over-quarter profit flow-through (down to 70% from 86%), but it's tied to a payoff that's already live, so it's not open-ended. On the upside: the AI improvement is measurable (the +58% per-install), and customer spending is expanding (28% above peak, very low churn after the first month).

**What the Q&A exposed:** under questioning, management's "it's just timing" softened into a more honest "this is unpredictable research." They also conceded the consumer ramp is harder and slower than the May hype implied. Analysts were probing, not cheering — consistent with the two downgrades that day.

**Net effect of the calls:** they cooled the hype but made the story more credible — reset expectations, cleared the SEC cloud, and gave a concrete, dated test. The clear catalyst is the **Q3 earnings report around November 4**. It genuinely matters (not just "more data") because it settles the one question the whole thesis hangs on: was Q2 a timing blip, or the start of a real slowdown? A number in or above guidance likely re-rates the stock up; a miss confirms the bears. About three months away.

## Bottom line

**The single most important correction:** AppLovin showed up on the screen with a high growth score partly because of a "+38.2 percentage point growth acceleration." **That's a data error, not reality.** On clean numbers, growth has *slowed every quarter for the last four* (+68% → +66% → +59% → +53%, down from a +77% peak). The growth *level* is still elite (over 50%); the "it's speeding up" signal was inverted by bad data. The entire debate now comes down to whether that slowdown levels off (management's "timing" story) or keeps going — and November's report will largely settle it.

**On the numbers:** a best-in-class, cash-rich machine — ~78% operating margins (still rising), ~66% free-cash-flow margins, 126% returns on capital, almost no net debt, and reported profits that are genuinely backed by cash. Quality is not in question. The only soft spot is that growth is decelerating and lumpy by management's own admission, with no backlog to give visibility.

**On the story:** it's reset from "unstoppable" to "still-great-but-slowing," with the SEC cloud gone. Analysts are trimming targets; institutions are buying; value investors think it's cheap. The catalyst is the November report, roughly three months out.

**What the price assumes:** at ~26x earnings, the market is pricing in a sharp, near-permanent slowdown for a company that just grew 53% at 66% margins with a pile of free upside. That's too pessimistic on *quality* — but not crazy on *growth uncertainty*, since management itself admitted the growth rate is lumpy and reset expectations lower. So there's a real gap between price and value, but it's not a screaming steal.

**Does the stock feed on itself (a doom loop)?** Not really. The usual vicious cycle — fear drives the price down, which hurts the business, which drives more fear — doesn't apply here, because AppLovin funds itself entirely from its own cash and doesn't need the stock market. A low stock price doesn't damage the business. So the risk of a self-reinforcing collapse is low; the turn depends on the November numbers, not on mood.

**Where it sits on AI:** firmly on the winning side. It's not an old software company under threat from AI — it *is* an AI company, already making money from it, with margins rising as it invests. The warning sign to watch would be several quarters in a row with no model improvement, or a rival's data eroding its edge.

**The bear case:** growth is admittedly lumpy research. If Q2 was the leading edge of a real slowdown, growth drops toward the ~30% long-term frame faster than expected and the stock gets re-rated to 15–18x → roughly **$195–235 (down 30–42%)**. The trigger to watch: Q3 revenue coming in below ~$2.0 billion (under the guidance floor), or the "already-live" improvement failing to compound. Secondary risks: the consumer ramp being slower than sold, the Singapore tax perk expiring in 2028 (−$0.80/share), and an Apple/Google rule change hurting ad targeting.

**The bull case:** elite economics at a cheap price — 78% margins, 66% cash-flow margins, 126% returns, a fortress balance sheet — at 26x while still growing over 50%, and the Q2 "miss" was under 1% with the fix already live. The trigger: Q3 landing in or above the $2.055–2.085 billion guide with consumer still compounding → the "timing" story is confirmed and the multiple re-rates toward 30–35x → roughly **$390–455 (up 16–36%)**. Plus all the free optionality (consumer, platform opening, lead-gen, connected-TV).

**Overall value assessment: a dollar for about 75–80 cents.** The three ingredients: the *financial quality* is strong (the main pillar), there's a *real, dated catalyst* (November), and the *narrative* is decent but mixed (value investors and institutions buying, but analysts trimming and the growth story just reset lower). Together that's a modestly positive setup — value is above price because the market is pricing a harsh slowdown into a demonstrably elite business — but it's not a slam-dunk bargain, because the growth question is genuinely open until November.

**What would prove the thesis wrong (watch these):**
1. Q3 revenue below ~$2.0 billion, or a second straight quarter with no model improvement → "timing" becomes "structural."
2. Operating margin dropping below ~72–73%, or free cash flow falling below ~55% of revenue → computing costs are structurally eating the economics.
3. MAX marketplace growth turning negative, or consumer ad spend falling quarter-over-quarter → demand is actually weakening.
4. An Apple or Google policy change that hurts ad targeting.
5. The Singapore tax perk confirmed dead for 2028 with nothing to offset it.

## Appendix: the growth-acceleration deep-dive (in plain terms)

**The goal:** figure out, on clean apples-to-apples numbers, how fast AppLovin's revenue grew in each of the last four quarters and whether that growth rate is speeding up or slowing down.

**The clean ad-business revenue, quarter by quarter ($ thousands):**

| Year | Q1 | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2024 | 678,370 | 711,015 | 835,186 | 999,487 |
| 2025 | 1,158,974 | 1,258,754 | 1,405,045 | 1,657,944 |
| 2026 | 1,842,449 | 1,923,686 | — | — |

*(These come from SEC filings and AppLovin's own press release for 2024, and from FMP/the 10-Q for 2025–26. The 2024 quarters add up exactly to the audited full-year figure of $3,224,058K, so we know they're right.)*

**The result:**

| Quarter | Revenue | Growth vs. a year earlier | Change in that growth rate | Growth vs. prior quarter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q3 2025 | $1,405M | **+68.2%** | −8.8 points | +11.6% |
| Q4 2025 | $1,658M | **+65.9%** | −2.4 points | +18.0% |
| Q1 2026 | $1,842M | **+59.0%** | −6.9 points | +11.1% |
| Q2 2026 | $1,924M | **+52.8%** | −6.2 points | +4.4% |

**What it means:** the growth rate has slowed every single one of the last four quarters — from a +77% peak (Q2 2025) down to +53% (Q2 2026), about 24 points of deceleration in a year. Some of that is just the math of getting bigger (harder to grow fast off a bigger base), and some is the lumpy model-improvement timing. The Q2 quarter-over-quarter growth of just +4.4% (versus +18% two quarters earlier) is the clearest sign of the model-timing air-pocket. Management's Q3 guide implies a bounce quarter-over-quarter but still a slowdown year-over-year — consistent with everything above.

**Why the screening file was wrong (proven exactly):** the file said growth *accelerated* +38.2 points. That number is a data artifact. The data provider (FMP) counts the year-ago Q4 2024 quarter as the *old, bigger* company (games included, $1,373M) instead of the ads-only company ($999M). Using the bloated old number, the prior quarter's growth looks like only +20.8% instead of the true +65.9%. So the file's math was: this quarter's +59% minus a fake +20.8% = +38.2 points of "acceleration" — which matches the file exactly, and is completely wrong. On clean numbers, that quarter actually *decelerated* by about 7 points. The lesson: don't trust the raw quarterly data for AppLovin across the 2024 games sale — use the SEC figures.

**The bottom line:** on honest, like-for-like numbers, AppLovin's growth is elite in *size* (over 50%) but clearly *slowing* in *rate* — the exact opposite of the "accelerating" signal that flagged it. That doesn't break the thesis (the business is still excellent and the growth is still fast), but it confirms the one thing that matters most: the growth rate is on a downward path, and November's earnings are the test of whether it steadies or keeps sliding.
