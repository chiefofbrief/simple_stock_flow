# Chapter 17 — Put Buying in Conjunction with Common Stock Ownership:
# Extracted Insights for the Conservative Options Playbook

## 1. The Protective Put: Structure, Cost, and the Insurance Analogy

"*The put purchase can be used to limit downside loss in a stock that is owned.* When one
simultaneously owns both the common stock and a put on that same stock, he has a
position with limited downside risk during the life of the put. This position is also called
a synthetic long call, because the profit graph is the same shape as a long call's."

"*The put functions much like an insurance policy with a finite life.*"

**Example:** An investor owns XYZ stock, which is at 52, and purchases an XYZ October
50 put for 2. The put gives him the right to sell XYZ at 50, so the most that the
stockholder can lose on his stock is 2 points. Since he pays 2 points for the put
protection, his maximum potential loss until October expiration is 4 points, no matter
how far XYZ might decline up until that time. If, on the other hand, the price of the stock
should move up by October, the investor would realize any gain in the stock, less the 2
points that he paid for the put protection.

**TABLE 17-1. Results at expiration on a protected stock holding.**

| XYZ Price at Expiration | Stock Profit | Put Profit | Total Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | −$2,200 | +$1,800 | −$400 |
| 40 | −1,200 | +800 | −400 |
| 50 | −200 | −200 | −400 |
| 54 | +200 | −200 | 0 |
| 60 | +800 | −200 | +600 |
| 70 | +1,800 | −200 | +1,600 |
| 80 | +2,800 | −200 | +2,600 |

Notice that if the stock were below 48 in October, the common stock owner would have
been better off buying the put. However, with XYZ above 48 at expiration, the put
purchase was a burden that cost a small portion of potential profits.

"This strategy, however, is not necessarily geared to maximizing one's profit potential on
the common stock, but rather provides the stock owner with *protection*, eliminating the
possibility of any devastating loss on the stock holding during the life of the put."

Two general classes of stock owners find this particularly attractive: First, the long-term
holder who is not considering selling the stock — whose cost basis may make the capital
gains tax on the sale prohibitive — may utilize the put to limit losses over a short-term
horizon while still allowing room for upside appreciation. Second, the investor who is
*establishing* a position in the stock may buy the put simultaneously with the stock. "He
immediately starts out with a position that has limited downside risk with large potential
profits if the stock moves up. In this way, he can feel free to hold the stock during the life
of the put without worrying about when to sell it if it should experience a temporary
setback." The stock owner who has a put for protection need not overreact to a downward
move. "He can afford to sit back and wait during the life of the put, since he has built-in
protection." This eliminates the necessity of placing a stop-loss order on the stock —
avoiding the frustrating outcome of a stop being triggered by a temporary dip, only for
the stock to subsequently rise.

> **Annotation:** For the value investor, the protective put is the cleanest expression of
> the playbook's core goal: maintain full upside participation in a thesis while capping
> the cost of being wrong. The two-class framework maps directly to the two primary
> playbook use cases — hedging an existing long that cannot be sold (tax basis,
> concentration) and initiating a new position with built-in protection during the early,
> highest-uncertainty phase of the thesis. The put-as-stop-loss substitute is particularly
> compelling: a stop-loss order exits the position permanently on a bad tick; a protective
> put keeps the investor in the position through temporary volatility while still defining
> the maximum loss.

## 2. Which Put to Buy for Protection: The Slightly OTM Strike Is the Target

The selection of which put to buy determines the trade-off between protection and profit
drag.

**OTM put — disaster insurance only:**

**Example:** XYZ is at 40 and the October 35 put is selling for .50. The purchase of this
put as protection would not reduce upside potential much at all, only by .50. However,
the stock owner could lose 5.50 points if XYZ fell to 35 or below. That is his maximum
possible loss, for if XYZ were below 35 at October expiration, he could exercise his put
to sell the stock at 35, losing 5 points on the stock, and he would have paid 50 cents for
the put, bringing his total loss to 5.50 points.

"*The purchase of the out-of-the-money put will not provide as much downside protection
as an at- or in-the-money put would.* The purchase of a deeply out-of-the-money put as
protection is more like 'disaster insurance': It will prevent a stock owner from
experiencing a disaster in terms of a downside loss during the life of the put, but will not
provide much protection in the case of a limited stock decline."

**Deep ITM put — overly conservative:**

**Example:** XYZ is again at 40 and there is an October 45 put selling for 5.50. The stock
owner who purchases the October 45 put would have a maximum risk of 50 cents, for he
could always exercise the put to sell stock at 45, giving him a 5-point gain on the stock,
but he paid 5.50 points for the put, thereby giving him an overall maximum loss of
50 cents. He would have difficulty making any profit during the life of the put, however.
XYZ would have to rise by more than 5.50 points (the cost of the put) for him to make
any total profit on the position by October expiration.

"*The deep in-the-money put purchase is overly conservative and is usually not a good
strategy.* On the other hand, it is not wise to purchase a put that is too deeply
out-of-the-money as protection. *Generally, one should purchase a slightly out-of-the-money
put as protection.* This helps to achieve a balance between the positive feature of
protection for the common stock and the negative feature of limiting profits."

The equivalence to call buying confirms this conclusion: "*Therefore, the call purchase
and the long put/long stock strategies are equivalent.*" In Chapter 3, it was determined
that the slightly in-the-money call often offers the best ratio between risk and reward.
"Similarly, the slightly out-of-the-money put often offers the best ratio between risk and
reward for the common stockholder who is buying the put for protection. Again, the stock
is slightly above the striking price. Actually, since the two positions are equivalent, the
same conclusions should be arrived at."

> **Annotation:** The strike selection rule for protective puts is therefore: buy the first
> OTM strike below the current stock price. This minimizes premium cost while keeping
> the protection floor close enough to current price to be meaningful. In McMillan's
> example with XYZ at 40, the October 35 put (12.5% OTM) is too far away to provide
> meaningful protection against a moderate decline; the correct strike would be the 40 or
> nearest OTM put. The deep ITM put (October 45 at 5.50 with XYZ at 40) eliminates
> almost all risk but also eliminates almost all profit potential.
>
> The synthetic equivalence has an additional practical implication: before buying stock
> plus a protective put, compare the total cost to simply buying a call outright at the
> equivalent strike. Long stock at 40 plus October 35 put at .50 costs approximately $40.50
> of capital at risk per share. A call with equivalent profit characteristics requires far less
> capital. The covered call writer with an ITM call substitute (as discussed in Chapter 7)
> may be the more capital-efficient structure when a long-term stock holding is not
> required.

## 3. Tax Warning: Buying a Put on a Short-Term Stock Holding Resets the Clock

"If the stock owner is already a long-term holder of the stock at the time that he buys the
put, the put purchase has no effect on his tax status. Similarly, if the stock buyer buys the
stock at the time that he buys the put and identifies the position as a hedge, there is no
effect on the tax status of his stock. However, *if one is currently a short-term holder of
the common stock at the time that he buys a put, he eliminates any accrued holding
period on his common stock. Moreover, the holding period for that stock does not begin
again until the put is sold.*"

**Example:** Assume the long-term holding period is 6 months. An investor who bought
the stock and held it for 5 months and then purchased a put would wipe out his entire
holding period of 5 months. Suppose he then held the put and the stock simultaneously
for 6 months, liquidating the put at the end of 6 months. His holding period would start
all over again for that common stock. Even though he has owned the stock for 11 months —
5 months prior to the put purchase and 6 months more while he simultaneously owned
the put — his holding period for tax purposes is considered to be zero!

> **Annotation:** Before buying a protective put on any existing stock holding, check
> the holding period. If the position has not yet crossed the long-term threshold, buying
> the put resets the clock entirely — potentially converting a long-term gain into a
> short-term gain on a future sale. The safe entry points are: (1) buy the put
> simultaneously with the stock and identify it as a hedge, or (2) buy the put only after
> the stock has already qualified for long-term treatment. Consult a tax advisor if there
> is any doubt.

## 4. The Collar (Protective Covered Write): Adding a Put to a Covered Call Position

"*The purchase of an out-of-the-money put option can eliminate the risk of large potential
losses for the covered write*, although the money spent for the put purchase will reduce
the overall return from the covered write. One must therefore include the put cost in his
initial calculations to determine if it is worthwhile to buy the put."

**Example:** XYZ is at 39. XYZ October 40 call: 3. XYZ October 35 put: .50.

A covered write is established by buying the common at 39 and selling the October 40
call for 3. This covered write has a maximum profit potential of 4 points if XYZ is
anywhere above 40 at expiration, and loses money if XYZ is anywhere below 36 (the
break-even point) at expiration.

By also purchasing the October 35 put, the results change as follows:
- Maximum profit potential is reduced from 4 points to 3.50 points.
- Break-even point moves up from 36 to 36.50.
- Maximum loss is now capped at 1.50 points if XYZ is below 35 at expiration.

**TABLE 17-2. Comparison of regular and protected covered writes.**

| XYZ Price at Expiration | Stock Profit | Oct 40 Call Profit | Oct 35 Put Profit | Total Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | −$1,400 | +$300 | +$950 | −$150 |
| 30 | −900 | +300 | +450 | −150 |
| 35 | −400 | +300 | −50 | −150 |
| 36.50 | −250 | +300 | −50 | 0 |
| 38 | −100 | +300 | −50 | +150 |
| 40 | +100 | +300 | −50 | +350 |
| 45 | +600 | −200 | −50 | +350 |
| 50 | +1,100 | −700 | −50 | +350 |

"The maximum risk is quite small and *the writer need never be forced to roll down in a
disadvantageous situation.*"

The key behavioral advantage: "The writer who has the protective put need not be
bothered with such things. He never has to roll down, for he has a limited maximum loss.
Therefore, he should never get into a 'locked-in' loss situation. This can be a great
advantage, especially from an emotional viewpoint, because the writer is never forced to
make a decision as to the future price of the stock in the middle of the stock's decline.
With the put in place, he can feel free to take no action at all, since his overall loss is
limited. If the stock should rally upward later, he will still be in a position to make his
maximum profit."

On the longer-term cost of buying puts with covered writes: "it would appear that the
writer reduces his overall rate of return slightly by buying the puts. This is because he
gives something away if the stock falls slightly, remains unchanged, or rises in price. He
only 'gains' something if the stock falls heavily. Since the odds of a stock falling heavily
are small in comparison to the other events, the writer will be gaining something in only
a small percentage of cases. However, the put buying strategy may still prove useful in
that it removes the emotional uncertainty of large losses."

This strategy (covered write + protective put) is equivalent to the bull spread: "Notice
that the profit graph in Figure 17-2 has the same shape as the bull spread profit graph.
This means that the two strategies are equivalent."

> **Annotation:** The collar is the covered write with its Achilles heel removed. The
> covered writer's exposure to a large stock decline forces roll-down decisions at exactly
> the wrong moment — when the stock is falling and emotions are running high. The
> protective put eliminates that forced decision entirely. The cost is a modest reduction
> in maximum profit (from 4 to 3.50 in the example) and a slightly higher break-even
> (36.50 vs. 36). For the value investor already writing covered calls against a long stock
> position, adding an OTM put when implied volatility is elevated — making the put
> cheaper relative to its historical cost — converts the covered write into a defined-risk
> structure at minimal additional cost. McMillan's note on long-term return drag is
> honest and important: the put only pays off in the tail scenario. The value is not in
> expected return but in removing the forced decision and the possibility of catastrophic
> loss.

## 5. The No-Cost Collar: Funding Put Protection by Selling an OTM Call

"If he buys an out-of-the-money put, it is quite possible that he might be able to sell an
out-of-the-money call whose proceeds completely cover the cost of the put. Thus, he has
established a protective collar at no cost — at least no debit. His 'cost' is the fact that he
has forsaken the upside profit potential on his stock, above the striking price of the
written call."

**Worked example with LEAPS:** In 1999, a company owned 5 million shares of Cisco
(CSCO) trading at about 130 with volatility of about 50%. A three-year put struck at 130
sold for about the same price as a three-year call struck at 200. Thus, the company was
able to hedge all of its CSCO stock with no downside risk (the put strike equaled the
current stock price) and still had profit potential of over 50% to the upside over the next
three years.

"*One should consider using LEAPS options when he establishes a collar* — even if he
is not an institutional trader — because the striking price of the calls can be quite high in
comparison to that of the put's strike or in comparison to the price of the underlying
stock."

**TABLE 17-3. Highest Call Strike That Pays for an At-the-Money Put
(Assuming 2.5 years to expiration)**

| Volatility of Underlying | Call Strike |
|---|---|
| 30% | 30% out of money |
| 40% | 35% out of money |
| 50% | 40% out of money |
| 70% | 50% out of money |
| 100% | 70% out of money |

The table shows that the higher the stock's volatility, the further OTM the written call
can be struck and still cover the cost of an ATM put. For a 50% volatility stock with 2.5
years to expiration, selling a call 40% out of the money fully covers the cost of buying an
ATM put.

> **Annotation:** For the value investor holding a concentrated or low-basis stock
> position, the no-cost collar using LEAPS is the most capital-efficient form of downside
> protection available. The key insight from Table 17-3 is that higher-volatility names —
> exactly the kind of beaten-down, undervalued stocks the value investor tends to own —
> generate the most favorable collar terms. A stock with 50% implied volatility allows the
> call strike to be set 40% above the current price before any out-of-pocket cost is
> incurred for the put. In practical terms: run the no-cost collar calculation before buying
> any protective put outright. If the required call strike is above a realistic price target
> for the stock, the no-cost collar is strictly superior to paying cash for the put.

## 6. The Partial Collar: Preserving Some Upside While Funding the Put

"One does not necessarily have to forsake all of the profit potential from his stock. He
might buy the puts, as usual, and then sell calls with a somewhat lower strike than needed
for a low-cost collar, but the quantity of calls sold would be less than that of stock owned.
In that way, there would be unlimited profit potential on *some* of the shares of the
underlying stock."

**Worked example:**

XYZ: 61; Apr 55 put: 1; Apr 65 call: 2.

Owner of 1,000 shares: purchase 10 Apr 55 puts at 1 point apiece ($1,000 total cost).
To cover the cost of those puts, sell only *five* Apr 65 calls at 2 points apiece ($1,000
credit). The protection costs nothing and there is still unlimited profit potential on 500
of the shares, since only five calls were sold against the 1,000 shares owned.

**Extended example comparing two call strikes:**

XYZ: 61; Apr 55 put: 1; Apr 65 call: 2; Apr 70 call: 1.

Option A: Sell five Apr 65 calls — unlimited profit on 500 shares, other 500 called away
at 65.
Option B: Sell ten Apr 70 calls — all shares capped at 70, but at a higher level.

"In the former strategy, if the stock climbs all the way to 75, it results in the same profit
as if the stock is called away at 70 in the latter strategy. This is true because 500 shares
would be worth 75, but the other 500 would have been called away at 65 — making for
an average of 70. Hence, the former strategy only outperforms the latter if the stock
actually climbs above 75."

"The tradeoff is that a lower call strike allows for more eventual upside profit potential,
but it limits what has been written against to a lower price."

> **Annotation:** The partial collar is the right structure when the investor wants
> meaningful downside protection but is unwilling to cap all upside at a single strike.
> Selling fewer calls than shares owned preserves unlimited participation on the
> uncovered shares. The key sizing decision: solve for the minimum number of calls to
> sell that covers the put cost, then check whether the resulting call strike is acceptably
> above the stock's price target. The two-strategy comparison shows that Option A
> (fewer calls at a lower strike) and Option B (more calls at a higher strike) produce
> identical results at XYZ = 75 — the choice between them is purely a function of whether
> the investor believes the stock will exceed 75, and McMillan's assessment is that this
> is a rather unlikely event.

## 7. Adjusting the Collar After a Large Move

**After a sharp stock decline:** "After the stock has dropped, the put would be worth a
considerable amount, while the call would be worth very little. If the investor felt that the
majority of the decline in the stock was finished, he could merely sell the put. Whether
or not he covered the call, that would leave him with large profit potential if the stock
should rally. On the other hand, if the investor is not certain that the stock has stopped
declining, he might roll just the put — or perhaps both the put and the call — down to
lower strikes, thereby taking a large credit out of the position in doing so (the credit comes
from selling the original put, which is now quite valuable). As a third choice, he could also
consider selling some out-of-money puts against the puts that are owned. This would
bring in some credit, but would expose the stock to losses below the striking price of the
short puts."

**After a sharp stock rally:** "If the underlying stock increases substantially in price after
the collar has been established, the only way out of the collar is to cover the written
calls — and that is going to require a (large) debit. Of course, the underlying stock has
risen in price, so that is an unrealized profit that could be used to offset the loss in the
calls. In essence, *there is no convenient exit strategy from a collar on the upside.*"

**Critical warning on call writing against stock that cannot be sold:** "If one sells options
against stock that he has no intention of selling, he is actually writing naked calls in his
own mind. That is, if one owns stock that 'can't' be sold — perhaps the capital gains
would be devastating or the stock has been 'in the family' for a long time — then he
should not sell covered calls against it, because he will be forced into treating the calls as
naked (if he refuses to sell the stock)."

> **Annotation:** The upside exit problem is the most underappreciated risk in the collar.
> When the stock rallies sharply above the short call strike, the investor faces a painful
> choice: pay a large debit to buy back the call (effectively giving back some of the stock
> gain to remove the cap), or accept assignment and sell the stock at the call strike. For
> the value investor who does not want to sell — especially in a low-basis position —
> this can be deeply uncomfortable. McMillan's warning about "naked calls in your own
> mind" is the most important sentence in the chapter for this investor profile: do not
> write calls against stock you cannot or will not sell. In that case, buy the put outright
> and pay the premium; do not try to fund it by capping a position you intend to hold
> indefinitely.
